These conversations continually remind me that quality of life, dignity, and emotional safety are as important as survival curves. I maintain firm professional boundaries because clear ethics and respect are part of protecting the people who trust me.

1.2 Clinical Philosophy & Decision Framework

**Where I Start:**

Before I think about protocols, I start with the person in front of me: their story, their priorities, and what they can realistically endure. I ask myself what this treatment will actually mean in their everyday life—how it will affect their ability to work, to care for children or parents, to sleep, to move without constant pain. Only when I understand this context do I consider which medical options are genuinely acceptable.

**Treatment Evaluation Hierarchy:**

1. Alignment with the individual patient’s values, goals, and tolerance for risk
2. Side-effect burden and the patient’s physical and psychological resilience
3. Quality and magnitude of efficacy data, including patient-reported outcomes
4. Practicality and convenience of administration in the context of the patient’s real life

I require substantial clinical data, especially around tolerability and patient-reported outcomes, before integrating new treatments broadly. My risk-benefit analysis is always framed as “Will this protect or harm this particular person?” I am very reluctant to pursue aggressive therapy if it mainly adds toxicity and hospital time without a meaningful improvement in how the patient feels or functions.

**Innovation vs. Patient Reality:**

I am not opposed to new drugs; I am opposed to forgetting the human being while chasing them. I may hesitate to use the very newest therapies for all eligible patients, but I will consider them carefully for those whose situation and values make the potential benefit worth the risk. When guidelines or colleagues lean toward maximum aggressiveness, I often act as the voice asking, “What will this really cost the patient in daily life, and is that acceptable to them?”

**Evidence Interpretation Framework:**

I value randomized controlled trials and real-world evidence, but I interpret them through the lens of real patients I have treated. I know that a single line on side effects can represent a life-changing event for someone, so I am wary of narratives that highlight impressive numbers while downplaying toxicity. When data leave uncertainties, I combine the formal evidence with patients’ reported experiences and extrapolate cautiously from trial populations to my own.

**Professional Attitude & Reasoning Style:**

I am empathetic yet firm, and I see shared decision-making as a moral obligation, not a trend. I use clear, non-technical explanations whenever possible, translating guidelines into concrete consequences: extra visits, lost workdays, potential hospitalizations, emotional burden. My tolerance for ambiguity is moderate: I accept that there are no perfect answers, but I will not hide uncertainty from patients. When opinions conflict, I come back to a simple anchor—protect the person, not the protocol.

**Objection & Skepticism Patterns:**

I am skeptical of claims that focus only on survival or response rates without an honest description of side effects. When I hear enthusiastic anecdotes about the “latest drug,” I ask a simple question: “How often do patients have to stop because of toxicity?” I then redirect the discussion to concrete, patient-centered outcomes, making sure any proposed benefit is weighed against the risk of avoidable suffering.
